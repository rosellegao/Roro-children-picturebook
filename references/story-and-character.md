# Story and character alignment

## Intake and sequencing

Before proposing story directions, resolve four inputs: the protagonist's identity, the kinds of characters or co-stars the child likes, the user's theme ideas, and a short introduction to the child. Ask for favorite cartoons or visual styles as preference signals, but do not lock detailed appearance or style yet. Never carry forward a protagonist, supporting cast, or visual world from an earlier book unless the user explicitly requests it for this book.

The protagonist's identity belongs before story selection because it shapes agency and relationships. Detailed visual design belongs after story selection because the chosen events determine useful clothing, props, expressions, and settings.

## Story target

Write for the specific child, usually around ages two to three, rather than for a generic “little kid.” Let personality shape what happens: an outgoing child may initiate a game; a cautious child may first observe; a talkative child may rehearse a sentence that becomes difficult to say at the real moment.

A strong spine can be said in one or two sentences and contains:

1. a wish or small difficulty the child understands;
2. a plan the child tries;
3. a setback that changes what happens next;
4. an action the child chooses;
5. a warm ending that proves the meaning.

The setback should do story work. “The ball missed the goal and rolled into a tunnel” is useful when retrieving it makes the characters cooperate and become friends. A random sequence of unrelated tasks followed by “I learned courage” is not.

## Story options

Use two levels of story choice rather than writing many complete drafts at once:

1. Start with about eight to ten concise, genuinely different directions. Keep each to one or two sentences that reveal the opening situation, central change, and emotional promise. Vary the underlying conflict, action, relationship, and ending—not just the location or prop.
2. Ask the user to shortlist two or three directions. Expand only those into comparable causal routes. For each shortlisted route, state:

- the child’s opening situation;
- the central change or complication;
- what the child does;
- the warm ending and implied meaning.

After the user chooses one route, prepare a compact alignment draft:

- one-sentence spine;
- beginning, turn, resolution, and closing image;
- page or scene outline;
- key dialogue in each character’s natural register;
- the evidence that makes the ending feel earned.

Read the draft aloud. Remove adult abstractions, repetitive exposition, and dialogue that merely explains the plot.

## Character bible

After the story route is chosen, ask whether the user has a photo that may be used as a visual reference or prefers to describe the protagonist. A photo is optional, not a prerequisite. For a child's photo, confirm that the user is authorized to provide and use it, keep its use limited to the requested work, and exclude source photos from delivery packages unless the user explicitly asks otherwise.

For every recurring character, record only features that affect continuity:

- apparent age and body proportions;
- face shape, eyes, hair, ears, skin or fur tone;
- clothing, shoes, bag, and stable accessories;
- personality expressed through posture and action;
- speech rhythm and vocabulary;
- scale relative to the other characters.

For a child based on photographs, preserve recognizable but non-photoreal features. Match the requested cartoon world rather than producing a realistic child pasted beside cartoon characters. Keep clothing and bags accurate when the user calls them out. Do not exaggerate a feature unless asked.

For an established fictional character, preserve the requested classic appearance and broad personality. If a story depends on a specific official fact, verify that fact instead of treating invented details from an earlier book as canon.

## Style brief

Ask which cartoons, characters, or illustrated books the child enjoys. Translate the answer into high-level visual properties that can be reviewed directly: line quality, palette, shapes and proportions, texture or rendering, background density, and emotional tone. Use those properties to create a coherent new visual world. Do not assume that naming a favorite cartoon requests a literal replica, the inclusion of its cast, or the reuse of its plot.

If the user explicitly wants an established character in the story, separate that cast decision from the broader style decision. Keep the requested character recognizable while still aligning the new protagonist and setting into one illustrated world.

## Visual sample gate

Before batch illustration, make one representative same-scene sample showing the new child with the recurring cast. Evaluate:

- whether all characters appear to belong to the same world;
- whether the child is recognizable at the chosen cartoon level;
- whether skin tone, hair, clothing, bag, and scale are right;
- whether the background remains legible and useful;
- whether the composition leaves room for the final text layout.

After approval, keep a page-by-page continuity sheet for clothing, props, time of day, locations, character positions, and emotional progression. Vary camera distance, action, and staging; do not reuse one composition across the book.
